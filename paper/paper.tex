\Shorttitle{Stress-Testing Chatterjee's Xi Correlation Coefficient}

\Abstract{
  Chatterjee's (2021) $\xi$ correlation coefficient promises to detect
  any form of functional dependence between two variables via a
  rank-based formula, together with a computationally cheap asymptotic
  independence test. Despite rapid growth in the theoretical literature
  on $\xi_n$ since its introduction, no prior study has subjected it to
  a Monte Carlo simulation design that systematically crosses sample
  size, variable type, distribution shape, and dependence geometry, nor
  compared it under the same conditions against distance correlation
  and copula-based measures. This article closes that gap through a
  modular design, each module grounded in an already-established
  property of $\xi_n$, motivated by psychological research, where
  constructs are multifactorial phenomena that are difficult to
  isolate. The results confirm a finite-sample bias that decreases with
  $n$, does not depend solely on the known algebraic bound, and is
  consistently larger under a high dependence regime than under a
  moderate one; distinct, non-additive costs of ties depending on
  whether they affect the predictor, the response, or both, attenuable
  through composite scores with markedly diminishing returns; a
  comparison with distance correlation across marginal-family changes
  that turned out more nuanced than expected --- $\xi_n$ more stable in
  its point estimate, but not uniformly so in bias or variance ---
  except in an isolated sub-experiment where $\xi_n$'s invariance under
  monotone transformations held without qualification; a dependence
  geometry that determines which statistic detects best --- with an
  unexpected finding in which distance correlation and a copula-based
  measure diverge substantially from each other under circular
  relationships, rather than behaving as a homogeneous block of
  alternatives; and empirical evidence, consistent with theory, of
  $\xi_n$'s own detection threshold weakening near independence
  relative to the optimal parametric rate expected away from it ---
  without itself comparing that threshold against competing measures.
  A real-data illustration with personality
  data reproduces two of these findings directly. Taken together, the
  results offer practical guidance on when $\xi_n$ should be preferred,
  interpreted with caution, or complemented with classical measures, in
  real psychological data.
}

\section{Introduction} \label{sec:introduction}

Quantifying the strength of a relationship between two variables is
one of the oldest and most recurring tasks in applied statistics.
However, the three coefficients that dominate practice --- Pearson,
Spearman, and Kendall --- share a well-known limitation: they are
designed to detect linear or, at most, monotone dependence, and can
approach zero even in the presence of a perfect, noiseless functional
relationship if that relationship is not monotone \citep{chatterjee2021}.
In the behavioral and social sciences, where relationships between
constructs are rarely known in advance and often involve thresholds,
ceilings, or U-shapes, this limitation is not a minor technicality.

This article seeks to contribute specifically to psychology, where
this limitation takes on particular relevance. Psychological
constructs --- personality, attitudes, symptoms, cognitions --- are
rarely the result of an isolable mechanism: they emerge from
multifactorial phenomena in which individual dispositions, learning
history, social context, and biological processes intervene
simultaneously, none of which can be experimentally controlled or
isolated with the cleanliness of a physical system. This multifactorial
nature implies that the functional form of the relationship between
two psychological variables is rarely known in advance, so that
assuming linearity or monotonicity is not a harmless simplification but
a bet on a mechanism that, by its very nature, is difficult to isolate
and verify. A coefficient that promises to detect any form of
functional dependence, such as $\xi_n$, is in principle the natural
tool for this situation --- but that same promise demands that it be
subjected to the conditions under which psychological data are
actually collected: ordinal scales with few points (Likert-type items),
sample sizes moderated by recruitment costs, and heterogeneous
populations that produce skewed distributions or mixtures. It is
exactly that combination of conditions that this study puts to the
test, and that is illustrated in Section~\ref{sec:realdata} with real
personality data.

This blindness to possible non-monotonicity is not limited to
bivariate correlation coefficients: even when standard psychometric
practice --- structural equation models, second-order factor
structures --- runs into unexpected relationships that force a model
to be respecified (cross-loadings, correlated residuals, additional
factors), that respecification almost always occurs within the same
paradigm of monotone or linear relationships between factors and
indicators: it is rarely questioned whether the underlying relationship
could be non-monotone at all. Systematically exploring that
possibility --- for instance, through simulated path analyses with
truly non-monotone coefficients --- exceeds the scope of this article
and is left as future work; here we focus on the bivariate case.

\citet{chatterjee2021} proposed an alternative that promises to solve
this problem with an almost trivial formula: a rank-based coefficient,
$\xi_n$, that converges to a population quantity equal to zero if and
only if the variables are independent, and equal to one if and only if
one is a measurable function of the other --- regardless of the shape
of that function --- and that further admits an independence test with
known asymptotic distribution, requiring no resampling. This
combination of simplicity, generality, and asymptotic tractability
explains why, in just a few years, $\xi_n$ has generated a rapidly
growing theoretical literature: extensions to conditional and
multivariate dependence \citep{azadkia2021}, bias corrections
\citep{dalitz2024}, generalized families \citep{gao2024}, continuity
results \citep{ansari2026, bucher2025}, and exact detection thresholds
\citep{auddy2024}.

Despite this volume of theoretical work, none of these studies --- nor
any other we have been able to locate --- subjects $\xi_n$ to a
simulation design --- factorial or otherwise --- that systematically
crosses sample size, variable type, distribution shape, dependence
structure, and dependence magnitude, nor compares it under those same
conditions against the historical benchmarks of nonlinear dependence
(distance correlation, copula-based measures). Existing simulations
are, at best, of very limited scope: a few fixed models, one or two
sample sizes, without varying variable type. This article fills that
gap --- but, as detailed in Section~\ref{sec:framework}, not as an
unguided exploratory sweep: each of the five design factors was chosen
because the very algebraic or asymptotic structure of $\xi_n$, already
established in the literature cited above, predicts or at least
allows a specific effect on its behavior. We formulate these
predictions as five hypotheses (H1--H5) before describing the
simulation design that puts them to the test.

It is important to state the scope of this study up front.
\citet{bucher2025} show, through adversarial constructions ("shuffles
of min"), that $\xi$ can have trivial power and that no meaningful
exact-level confidence intervals exist for $\xi$ in the worst case. Our
study does not seek to refute or replicate those worst-case results ---
we take them as established and settled --- but rather to quantify,
through simulation, the behavior of $\xi_n$ under \emph{typical},
non-adversarial conditions, of the kind an applied researcher actually
encounters: skewed or heavy-tailed distributions, ordinal Likert-type
variables, moderate sample sizes, and realistic dependence structures.

The rest of the article is organized as follows. Section~\ref{sec:framework}
presents the theoretical framework for $\xi_n$, its competing measures,
and the study's five hypotheses. Section~\ref{sec:design} describes
the simulation's modular design --- hypothesis-driven, not a full
factorial (Section~\ref{sec:design}, 3.1) ---, the data-generating
mechanisms, and the outcome metrics. Section~\ref{sec:results} reports
the results organized by hypothesis. Section~\ref{sec:realdata}
illustrates the findings by applying $\xi_n$ and its competitors to a
real psychology dataset. Sections~\ref{sec:discussion}
and~\ref{sec:conclusion} discuss the practical implications for
applied researchers and conclude.

\section{Theoretical framework} \label{sec:framework}

\subsection{Chatterjee's xi coefficient}

The three classical measures of association --- Pearson's $r$,
Spearman's $\rho$, and Kendall's $\tau$ --- are effective at detecting
linear or monotone dependence, but can approach zero even in the
presence of a perfect, noiseless, non-monotone functional relationship
between $X$ and $Y$ \citep{chatterjee2021}. \cite{chatterjee2021}
proposed a rank-based coefficient that overcomes this restriction,
retaining the simplicity and asymptotic tractability of the classical
coefficients.

Let $(X_1,Y_1), \dots, (X_n,Y_n)$ be i.i.d.\ copies of $(X,Y)$, with
$Y$ not almost surely constant. Reorder the pairs so that
$X_{(1)} \le \cdots \le X_{(n)}$ (ties among the $X_i$ broken at
random), let $r_i$ be the rank of $Y_{(i)}$ among all the $Y_j$'s, and
let $l_i$ be the number of $j$ such that $Y_j \ge Y_{(i)}$. Chatterjee's
correlation coefficient is defined as
\begin{equation} \label{eq:xi}
  \xi_n(X,Y) := 1 - \frac{n \sum_{i=1}^{n-1} |r_{i+1} - r_i|}
                         {2 \sum_{i=1}^{n} l_i (n - l_i)},
\end{equation}
which, in the absence of ties among the $Y_i$'s, simplifies to
$\xi_n(X,Y) = 1 - 3\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}|r_{i+1}-r_i| / (n^2-1)$.

\cite{chatterjee2021} shows that $\xi_n(X,Y)$ converges almost surely,
as $n \to \infty$, to a deterministic limit
\begin{equation} \label{eq:xi-limit}
  \xi(X,Y) := \frac{\int \mathrm{Var}\big(E(\mathbf{1}_{\{Y \ge t\}} \mid X)\big)\, d\mu(t)}
                    {\int \mathrm{Var}\big(\mathbf{1}_{\{Y \ge t\}}\big)\, d\mu(t)},
\end{equation}
where $\mu$ is the law of $Y$. The limit satisfies $\xi(X,Y) \in
[0,1]$, with $\xi(X,Y) = 0$ if and only if $X$ and $Y$ are independent,
and $\xi(X,Y) = 1$ if and only if there exists a measurable function
$f$ such that $Y = f(X)$ almost surely --- regardless of whether $f$ is
linear, monotone, or oscillatory. No distributional assumption on
$(X,Y)$ is required, and $\xi_n$ is computable in $O(n \log n)$ time.

Three properties of $\xi_n$ are directly relevant to the design of the
simulation study that follows.

\emph{Directionality.} Unlike Pearson's, Spearman's, or Kendall's
coefficients, $\xi_n$ is not symmetric in $X$ and $Y$:
$\xi_n(X,Y) \ne \xi_n(Y,X)$ in general. This is by design ---
$\xi_n(X,Y)$ quantifies how well $Y$ is determined by $X$, not a
mutual, symmetric association \citep{chatterjee2021}.

\emph{Finite-sample bias.} Although $\xi_n$ converges to $\xi \in
[0,1]$, its maximum attainable value for finite $n$ is strictly less
than one. \cite{dalitz2024} show that, in the absence of ties among
the $Y_i$'s, $\xi_n(\vec x, \vec y) \le (n-2)/(n+1)$ for every
permutation $\vec x$ of the covariate --- for $n=20$ this bound is only
$\approx 0.86$. Because this bound is a consequence of the algebraic
structure of \eqref{eq:xi} rather than of noise in the data, it
guarantees a systematic downward bias of $\xi_n$ as an estimator of
$\xi$ whenever $\xi$ is close to one, an effect that should shrink as
$n$ grows.

\emph{Distribution-dependent variance under ties.} Under independence
of $X$ and $Y$, $\sqrt{n}\,\xi_n(X,Y)$ is asymptotically normal with
mean zero \citep{chatterjee2021}. If $Y$ is continuous, the limiting
variance is a universal constant, $2/5$, free of the law of $Y$
(Theorem 2.1). If $Y$ is \emph{not} continuous, however, the limiting
variance $\tau^2$ depends on the law of $Y$ (Theorem 2.2); Chatterjee
computes $\tau^2 = 1$ for $Y \sim \mathrm{Bernoulli}(1/2)$, exactly 2.5
times the continuous-case value. Moreover, when the $X_i$'s contain
ties, $\xi_n(X,Y)$ is itself a randomized statistic, since its value
depends on how ties are broken. These are not empirical curiosities but
proven consequences of \eqref{eq:xi}, and they bear directly on the
discrete and ordinal variables of the kind routinely analyzed in the
behavioral and social sciences.

\subsection{Competing measures of nonlinear dependence}

Two other families of statistics have historically pursued the same
goal --- detecting dependence beyond linearity or monotonicity --- via
different mathematical routes, and are the natural benchmarks against
which $\xi_n$ should be evaluated.

\emph{Distance correlation.} \cite{szekely2007} defined distance
covariance and distance correlation (dCor) from the pairwise distances
within each sample, showing that dCor$(X,Y) = 0$ if and only if $X$
and $Y$ are independent, for random vectors $X$ and $Y$ of arbitrary
dimension. dCor is symmetric and multivariate by construction, but,
because it operates on raw distances rather than ranks, it is not
invariant under nonlinear monotone reparametrizations of the marginals
in the way that $\xi_n$, Spearman's $\rho$, or Kendall's $\tau$ are.
Its naive computational cost is $O(n^2)$ (a fast $O(n \log n)$
algorithm exists only for the univariate case), and independence
testing generally requires a permutation test rather than a tractable
null distribution.

\emph{Copula-based measures.} \cite{hoeffding1948} proposed one of the
earliest general nonparametric tests of independence, a rank-based
$U$-statistic (Hoeffding's $D$) measuring the discrepancy between the
joint empirical distribution of $(X,Y)$ and the product of its
marginals. $D$ is symmetric and consistent against broad alternatives,
but has a known blind spot: certain dependent, symmetric configurations
(e.g., $Y = |X|$-type relationships) can drive $D$ close to zero
despite genuine dependence. \cite{bergsma2014} introduced $\tau^\ast$,
a related sign-covariance statistic designed to be strictly positive
whenever $X$ and $Y$ are dependent, correcting this deficiency. Both
statistics are $U$-statistics of order four or five, with a naive
computational cost of $O(n^2)$ or worse.

\subsection{Hypothesized effects of sample size, variable type, distribution shape, dependence structure, and dependence regime}

The simulation design of Section~\ref{sec:design} is not an unguided
sweep of arbitrary conditions: each hypothesis was chosen because the
algebraic or asymptotic structure of $\xi_n$ reviewed above predicts,
or at least allows, a specific effect on its behavior. Several of the
hypotheses originally posed as a single effect turned out, after
closer scrutiny, to conflate distinct mechanisms that require separate
designs and estimands; they are presented here already disaggregated.

\begin{description}
\item[H1 (sample size).] The finite-sample upper bound $(n-2)/(n+1)$
  (Section 2.1) shows, in a purely algebraic way, that $\xi_n$
  \emph{cannot} approach one in small samples when the true population
  dependence is high --- but that bound does not by itself imply that
  the bias is substantial for any value of $\xi$, nor that its pattern
  is the same under every dependence structure: for $\xi \approx 0.5$,
  for instance, the bound of $0.86$ at $n=20$ does not directly
  constrain the estimator. We therefore formulate H1 as a conditional
  hypothesis: the negative bias and the MSE of $\xi_n$ will generally
  decrease as $n$ increases, with a particularly marked reduction when the
  population value of $\xi$ is high; the magnitude --- and sign --- of
  the residual bias in other regimes must be verified empirically and
  may depend on the dependence structure and the pattern of ties (H2,
  H4). The interaction between $n$ and the true dependence level is
  thus substantive to H1, not only to H5.
\item[H2 (ties and variable type).] The original formulation grouped
  under a single label two proven mechanisms that are, in fact,
  distinct questions: ties in $Y$ alter $\xi_n$'s asymptotic variance
  ($\tau^2$ depends on the law of $Y$ when it is not continuous,
  Section 2.1), while ties in $X$ make $\xi_n$ a statistic randomized
  by tie-breaking. These are disaggregated into four sub-hypotheses:
  \begin{description}
  \item[H2a (ties in the response).] Holding $X$ continuous,
    discretizing $Y$ will increase or alter the variability of
    $\xi_n$ and may change the independence test's performance
    relative to the continuous case.
  \item[H2b (ties in the predictor).] Holding $Y$ continuous,
    discretizing $X$ will introduce additional variability
    attributable to random tie-breaking and will reduce the
    reproducibility of $\xi_n$; this effect will be larger with fewer
    categories and smaller samples.
  \item[H2c (joint discretization).] When both marginals are discrete,
    $\xi_n$'s behavior will reflect the combination of both
    mechanisms and will not be recoverable by simple extrapolation
    from H2a and H2b.
  \item[H2d (composite score).] In real psychometric practice, a
    construct is rarely measured with a single item: several
    (Likert-type) items measuring it with independent measurement
    error are aggregated into a composite score (e.g., the average).
    Aggregating $k$ items is expected to substantially attenuate the
    tie problem documented in H2a--H2c relative to a single raw item,
    though without eliminating it, since the composite score retains
    a finite number of possible values.
  \end{description}
  One interpretive clarification is in order: that discretization
  "preserves the underlying dependence structure" holds with respect
  to the latent mechanism, but does not imply that it preserves the
  same \emph{observed} value of $\xi$ --- discretizing transforms the
  observed joint distribution, and with it $\xi$ can change, which is
  particularly relevant given $\xi_n$'s directional character.
\item[H3 (distribution shape).] $\xi_n$'s invariance under strictly
  monotone transformations of the marginals would, in principle,
  suggest stability with respect to distribution shape. However,
  \cite{ansari2026} show that $\xi$ is \emph{not} continuous with
  respect to weak convergence in general, and that its continuity has
  only been established for specific families (elliptical,
  $\ell_1$-symmetric); \cite{bucher2025} take this to the extreme with
  adversarial constructions ("shuffles of min") that fall outside the
  scope of this study (Section~\ref{sec:introduction}). However,
  varying the marginal family within an additive model
  $Y=f(X)+\sigma E$ does not cleanly isolate the effect of marginal
  shape: it also changes the error distribution, the copula induced
  between $X$ and $Y$, the population value of $\xi$ before and after
  calibration, and the relative difficulty of the problem for dCor. We
  reformulate H3 accordingly: after calibrating each condition to the
  same population level of directional dependence
  (Section~\ref{sec:design}), we evaluate whether $\xi_n$'s bias,
  variance, and power change systematically across marginal families;
  differences are interpreted as sensitivity to the \emph{joint
  generating mechanism} associated with each family, not exclusively
  as sensitivity to the marginal. We further distinguish, within H3,
  two formally separate exercises: (i) a \emph{validation}, possible
  only for the normal family, for which \citet{ansari2026} provide an
  exact closed form --- if the simulation does not reproduce it, we do
  not proceed to (ii); no analogous closed form exists for $t$ with
  this study's additive design (Section~\ref{sec:results}, Validation),
  so that family is reported with the same status as the non-elliptical
  ones; and (ii) an \emph{empirical robustness study} with the design's
  non-elliptical families (exponential, bimodal mixture), where the
  genuine gap resides. Finally, since the comparison with dCor seeks to
  illustrate a difference in invariance (ranks vs.\ raw distances), and
  since changing marginal family via a new additive model does
  \emph{not} amount to applying a monotone transformation to the same
  pair $(X,Y)$, that property is better isolated in a dedicated
  sub-experiment (Section~\ref{sec:design}) than in the main factorial.
\item[H4 (dependence geometry).] \cite{chatterjee2021} and
  \cite{gao2024} already report that $\xi_n$ is more powerful than dCor
  for oscillatory, non-monotone relationships, but less powerful for
  linear, circular, or heteroskedastic relationships. However, these
  five structures do not represent variations on a single kind of
  dependence: linear, quadratic, and sinusoidal have a directional
  functional component from $X$ to $Y$; circular is not a globally
  single-valued function of $X$; and heteroskedastic exhibits
  conditional (dispersion) dependence that can be strong even when
  classical association measures are small. We reformulate H4 as a
  hypothesis about \emph{geometry}, not just functional form: the
  relative power ordering among $\xi_n$, dCor, and copula-based
  statistics will depend on the geometry of the dependence --- $\xi_n$
  should be especially competitive for directional, non-monotone
  relationships that are functionally informative from $X$ to $Y$;
  dCor should retain an advantage for symmetric, circular
  configurations, or those dominated by conditional dispersion. This
  power comparison is only interpretable if the tests being compared
  have empirical Type I error comparable to the nominal level under
  each condition (Section~\ref{sec:design}, 3.3); otherwise, "higher
  power" may simply be an artifact of calibration.
\item[H5 (dependence regime).] The original formulation conflated two
  distinct questions. \cite{auddy2024} show that, under \emph{local
  alternatives} --- sequences in which the signal magnitude shrinks
  with $n$ at a specific rate ---, $\xi_n$'s detection threshold is
  suboptimal ($n^{-1/4}$) for testing pure independence ($\xi_0=0$) but
  minimax optimal ($n^{-1/2}$) for testing degree of association
  ($\xi_0>0$). A design with \emph{fixed} levels of $\xi_0$ across all
  sample sizes --- as originally proposed --- is useful for studying
  bias, MSE, and practical power curves, but does not directly test
  that result on local alternatives, for which the signal must depend
  explicitly on $n$. Two sub-hypotheses are therefore distinguished:
  \begin{description}
  \item[H5a (practical performance at fixed dependence).] At fixed
    population values $\xi_0 \in \{0.1,0.3,0.5,0.8\}$, power will
    increase with $n$, but the rate of increase will depend on the
    dependence structure (H4) and the presence of ties (H2).
  \item[H5b (local sensitivity).] Under a sequence of alternatives
    whose signal shrinks as $n$ grows (following the framework of
    \citealp{auddy2024}), $\xi_n$'s own power will behave differently
    depending on whether the signal shrinks at $\xi_n$'s own
    ($n^{-1/4}$) or at the classical parametric ($n^{-1/2}$) rate,
    reflecting the theoretical asymmetry in its detection threshold
    near independence.
  \end{description}
  For H5a, the level $\xi_0 \approx 0$ requires an explicit condition
  of exact independence ($X \perp Y$ generated directly), distinct
  from the "near independence" with shrinking signal reserved for
  H5b --- a simply very large $\sigma$ is not equivalent to exact
  independence for the purpose of evaluating Type I error.
\end{description}

\section{Simulation design} \label{sec:design}

\subsection{Modular design}

A full factorial over the five axes of Section~\ref{sec:framework}
($n$, variable type, marginal shape, structure, regime) --- and their
not-always-explicit additional factors, such as which marginal is
discretized (H2a/b/c) or directionality $\xi_n(X,Y)$ vs.\
$\xi_n(Y,X)$ --- would result in a matrix of thousands of cells,
computationally unmanageable given the $O(n^2)$ or worse cost of the
competitors (Section~\ref{sec:design}, 3.4) and, more importantly,
unnecessary: each hypothesis requires only one or two factors to vary,
not all at once. Rather than a full factorial later reduced for
computational reasons, the design is structured from the outset as
hypothesis-driven modules, each with its own question and its own
factors:

\begin{table}[H]
\caption{\label{tab:modules}Modules of the simulation design.}
\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{p{1.6cm}p{4.6cm}p{5.3cm}}
\toprule
Module & Main question & Factors that vary \\
\midrule
Validation & Does the implementation reproduce cases with a known analytical solution? & $n$; elliptical families (normal, $t$); linear structure \\[4pt]
H1 & How does $\xi_n$'s bias/MSE evolve with $n$? & $n \times$ $\xi_0$ regime \\[4pt]
H2a--d & What effect do ties in $Y$, in $X$, in both, and their attenuation through item aggregation have? & number of categories $\times$ discretized margin (Y/X/both) $\times$ $n$; $B$ tie-breaks per dataset; number of items $k$ for H2d \\[4pt]
H3 & Is there sensitivity to non-elliptical distributional mechanisms, after calibrating to equal dependence? & marginal shape $\times$ reference structure; isolated monotone-invariance sub-experiment \\[4pt]
H4 & Which statistic best detects each dependence geometry? & statistic $\times$ structure $\times$ intensity; Type I error checked before comparing power \\[4pt]
H5a & How does practical performance at fixed dependence change? & $n \times$ fixed $\xi_0$ \\[4pt]
H5b & How does the detection threshold change under local alternatives? & $n \times$ signal shrinking in $n$ (1-2 structures) \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\end{table}

For each relevant cell, $\xi_n$, dCor, $\tau^\ast$, and --- as applied
benchmarks in routine use, not as general independence competitors ---
Pearson and Spearman are computed. Hoeffding's $D$ (Section~\ref{sec:framework})
is discussed only as theoretical background motivating $\tau^\ast$'s
design and is not itself among the statistics evaluated in any module:
$\tau^\ast$ was specifically designed to correct a known blind spot of
$D$, so it is prioritized throughout as the historical/copula-based
competitor rather than running both under a fixed computational
budget.

\subsection{Data-generating mechanisms}

\subsubsection{Dependence structure (H4)}

For a given marginal of $X$ (H3) and a noise level $\sigma \ge 0$, we
generate $Y$ according to the additive model $Y = f(X) + \sigma E$,
with $E$ independent of $X$ and of unit variance (its distribution
follows the same family assigned to $X$'s marginal under that
condition, except for the circular and heteroskedastic structures, see
below). The five functional forms $f$ are:

\begin{align*}
\text{Linear:} &\quad f(X) = X, \\
\text{Quadratic:} &\quad f(X) = X^2, \\
\text{Sinusoidal:} &\quad f(X) = \sin(2\pi X), \\
\text{Circular:} &\quad Y = Z\sqrt{1-X^2} + \sigma E, \quad Z = \pm 1 \text{ with prob.\ } 1/2 \text{ (independent of $X$)}, \\
\text{Heteroskedastic:} &\quad Y = 3\big(s(X)(1-\lambda)+\lambda\big)E, \quad s(X) = \mathbf{1}(|X|\le 0.5),
\end{align*}

Each structure has an intuitive analog in real psychological
phenomena --- mentioned here purely as interpretive aid for the
reader, without modifying the generating mechanism or representing a
substantive claim about any particular construct:

\begin{description}
\item[Linear] --- analogous to a simple dose-response relationship, e.g., between an intervention and symptom reduction.
\item[Quadratic] --- analogous to phenomena with an optimal point, e.g., activation/performance (the Yerkes--Dodson law), where both too little and too much activation reduce performance.
\item[Sinusoidal] --- analogous to cyclical phenomena, e.g., circadian or seasonal mood variation.
\item[Circular] --- analogous to a relationship of intensity with no preferred direction, e.g., the magnitude of an emotional response regardless of its valence (positive or negative).
\item[Heteroskedastic] --- analogous to greater response variability under certain conditions, e.g., more dispersion in reported affect under high stress than under low stress.
\end{description}

Only the circular and heteroskedastic structures require $X$ to have
bounded support --- so that $\sqrt{1-X^2}$ and the indicator
$\mathbf{1}(|X|\le 0.5)$ are well defined --- \emph{not} all five
structures alike. For these two structures specifically, $X$ is
obtained by applying to the latent variable $Z$ (of the marginal
family assigned by H3) a bounded monotone transformation, $X := 2F_Z(Z)-1
\in (-1,1)$, which preserves the order of $Z$; the "marginal shape"
compared in H3 refers, in these two cases, to that of $Z$ before that
transformation, not to that of $X$ as already observed and bounded.
For the linear, quadratic, and sinusoidal structures --- the only
structure family that H3 uses as its reference,
Section~\ref{sec:results} --- $X$ directly retains its original
marginal family, without forced rescaling, so that "normal", "$t$",
etc.\ refer in those cases to the distribution actually observed. The
circular and heteroskedastic forms follow the alternatives of
\citet[Section 4.3]{chatterjee2021}, used there precisely to exhibit
conditions where $\xi_n$ is \emph{less} powerful than dCor (H4); the
first three follow \citet{dalitz2024} and \citet{gao2024}.

\subsubsection{Calibrating the dependence regime (H1, H5a)}

The noise level $\sigma$ does not carry the same meaning for $\xi$
across structures --- the same $\sigma$ can correspond to very
different values of $\xi(X,Y)$ depending on the shape of $f$. For this
reason, for each (structure, marginal shape) combination, $\sigma$ is
calibrated numerically rather than fixed a priori, subject to three
precautions.

\emph{Monotonicity is not guaranteed.} A bisection search presumes
that $\xi(X,Y)$ increases monotonically as $\sigma$ decreases; this is
not guaranteed for every structure, in particular the circular, the
heteroskedastic, or after discretization. Before adopting bisection as
a general procedure, we empirically verify --- for each relevant
combination of structure and marginal shape --- the curve
$\sigma \mapsto \xi(X,Y)$ over a grid of values; if the curve is not
monotone over the relevant range, bisection is replaced by a global
search over that grid.

\emph{Calibration scale.} For H2's conditions (discretized variables),
calibration is performed on the continuous latent variable,
\emph{before} discretization --- not on the observed variable. This
allows us to study how much discretization distorts a common
underlying dependence; the resulting $\xi$ value on the observed scale
(after discretization) is computed and reported separately, and is not
imposed as the calibration target.

\emph{No shared population reference across statistics.} $\xi_n$,
dCor, and $\tau^\ast$ do not estimate the same population functional
nor share a scale, so calibrating to a value $\xi_0$ fixes the
(approximate) dependence level for $\xi_n$, but does not guarantee a
comparable level for dCor or $\tau^\ast$ under the same condition.
Consequently (Section~\ref{sec:design}, 3.3), the bias and MSE of each
statistic are reported relative to its \emph{own} population value ---
approximated via the same large-sample Monte Carlo procedure
($n=10^5$; using \citet{ansari2026}'s closed form as a shortcut and
check for H3's elliptical shapes) --- and comparison \emph{between}
statistics is limited to quantities comparable across scales: Type I
error, power, standardized effect size, and computation time.

The resulting value of $\sigma$ (and, where applicable, the $\xi$
observed after discretization) for each design cell is documented in
the reproducibility repository (Section~\ref{sec:design}, 3.4) along
with the calibration script.

\subsubsection{Discretization, nested tie-breaking, and composite scoring (H2a--d)}

Starting from the continuous latent variable generated as above, the
binary and ordinal versions (Likert-3, Likert-5, Likert-7) are obtained
by applying the theoretical cumulative distribution function of the
corresponding marginal to cut the range into $c \in \{2,3,5,7\}$
equal-probability categories, assigning integer codes $1,\dots,c$.
This preserves the latent dependence \emph{mechanism} --- not
necessarily the same \emph{observed} value of $\xi$, which can change
upon discretization (Section~\ref{sec:framework}, H2). Discretization
is applied, depending on the module, to $Y$ (H2a), to $X$ (H2b), or to
both (H2c), precisely so as not to conflate the effect of ties in the
covariate with the effect of ties in the response.

For conditions with ties in $X$ (H2b, H2c), a single Monte Carlo
replicate per dataset is not enough, because $\xi_n$ depends on random
tie-breaking. A nested design is used instead: (i) a latent dataset is
generated; (ii) it is discretized; (iii) $\xi_n$ is computed under $B$
different random tie-breaks of the same discretized dataset; and (iv)
$\xi_n$'s total variance is decomposed into a component \emph{between}
datasets (ordinary sampling variability) and a component \emph{within}
each dataset (variability induced by tie-breaking). This decomposition
--- planned as its own result in Section~\ref{sec:results} --- makes
it possible to distinguish ordinary sampling instability from the
instability introduced by the statistic's implementation under ties.

For H2d, a latent trait $\theta$ generates $k=10$ six-point Likert
items, $I_j = \mathrm{discretize}(\theta + \epsilon_j, 6)$ for
$j=1,\dots,k$, with $\epsilon_j$ independent measurement error across
items (same discretization procedure as H2a--c); the observed score is
$\bar{I} = \frac{1}{k}\sum_j I_j$. The parameters ($k=10$, 1--6 scale)
are chosen to match the typical length of a \texttt{bfi} facet
(Section~\ref{sec:realdata}). This is deliberately a simplification of
real psychometric practice, where there are usually many more items
(e.g., 40) grouped into several theoretically related factors, so that
the relationships among the resulting composite scores are complex
precisely because of the shared variance across correlated factors ---
exactly the kind of multifactorial phenomenon, difficult to isolate,
that motivates this study (Section~\ref{sec:introduction}). H2d is
limited to showing how item aggregation alone --- without that
additional multifactorial complexity yet --- already attenuates the
tie problem of H2a--c; exploring the full multifactorial structure
exceeds the scope of this article (Section~\ref{sec:introduction}).

\subsubsection{Invariance under monotone transformations (H3 sub-experiment)}

To cleanly isolate the invariance property discussed in H3 ---
distinct from comparing marginal families generated by separate
additive models (Section~\ref{sec:framework}) --- a sub-experiment
independent of the main H3 module is run: a single base joint
distribution $(X,Y)$ is generated, strictly monotone (but nonlinear)
transformations are applied to one or both marginals, and the
stability of $\xi_n$, Spearman's $\rho$, and Kendall's $\tau$ (which
should remain invariant) is compared against the numerical drift
expected in dCor (which is not). By keeping the underlying joint
distribution fixed and varying only the parametrization, this
sub-experiment tests the mathematical invariance property much more
directly than H3's main factorial.

\subsubsection{Local alternatives and exact independence (H5b)}

For the H5b module, one or two structures from the H4 module (linear
and sinusoidal) are selected, and, following the framework of
\citet{auddy2024}, a sequence of alternatives is defined whose signal
shrinks with $n$ at an explicit rate --- e.g., $c_n \propto n^{-1/4}$
and $c_n \propto n^{-1/2}$ --- rather than a fixed dependence level,
precisely so as to observe whether $\xi_n$'s empirical detection
threshold more closely resembles one order or the other. For the
evaluation of Type I error in H1/H5a, an explicit condition of exact
independence is additionally generated ($X \perp Y$, generated
directly rather than via a large $\sigma$), reserving the signal that
shrinks in $n$ for H5b's study of local power near independence.

\subsection{Outcome metrics}

For each condition, replicated $R$ times:

\begin{description}
\item[Bias and MSE] of each statistic relative to \emph{its own}
  theoretical population value --- $\xi$ for $\xi_n$, the population
  dCor for dCor, and so on (Section~\ref{sec:design}, 3.2) --- never
  relative to a single "population reference" shared across
  statistics, since they do not estimate the same functional nor share
  a scale (H1, H3).
\item[Variance decomposition] between datasets and within each dataset
  by random tie-breaking, for conditions with ties in the covariate
  (H2b, H2c).
\item[Empirical Type I error] at the nominal level (e.g., $0.05$),
  evaluated \emph{only} under the condition of exact independence
  ($X \perp Y$ generated directly, H1, H5a) --- never under
  alternatives, local or otherwise, since there the rejection
  proportion is power, not Type I error. The number of permutations
  used (if applicable) and the fixed random seed are reported alongside
  each results table; the $p$-value rule and the internal handling of
  ties otherwise follow each package's own defaults (\pkg{XICOR}'s
  asymptotic normal test for $\xi_n$; \pkg{energy}'s and
  \pkg{TauStar}'s permutation tests for dCor and $\tau^\ast$,
  respectively) and are not re-derived here.
\item[Power], evaluated under fixed dependence (H5a, $\xi_0>0$) and
  under the sequence of local alternatives (H5b), reported \emph{only}
  among statistics whose Type I error is reasonably controlled under
  the same condition --- otherwise the power comparison is an artifact
  of calibration, not a real comparison (H4). Power is comparable
  across $\xi_n$, dCor, and $\tau^\ast$ because all three are read as a
  rejection probability under the same generating condition, not
  because the coefficients share a scale or population functional
  (Section~\ref{sec:design}, 3.2).
\item[Computation time], given that $\xi_n$'s speed advantage is one of
  its most cited properties (Section~\ref{sec:framework},
  Table~\ref{tab:comparison}).
\end{description}

The interactions between factors --- bias/MSE against $n$ by $\xi_0$
level, power against dependence intensity by structure, Type I error by
variable type and number of categories, and the variance decomposition
between sampling and tie-breaking --- are reported as tables rather
than figures throughout Section~\ref{sec:results}, consistent with the
precision needed to compare specific cells across modules; the
calibration curves $\sigma \mapsto \xi(X,Y)$ by structure
(Section~\ref{sec:design}, 3.2) that document the monotonicity check
are likewise available, cell by cell, in the reproducibility
repository rather than reproduced here.

\subsection{Software and reproducibility}

The entire simulation is implemented in \proglang{R} (version
$\ge 4.3$), using the packages \pkg{XICOR} \citep[for $\xi_n$; the same
package used by][]{chatterjee2021,dalitz2024}, \pkg{energy} \citep[for
dCor; by the same authors who proposed it,][]{szekely2007},
\pkg{TauStar} (for $\tau^\ast$, prioritized as the main rank/copula
competitor, Section~\ref{sec:design}, 3.1), and \proglang{R}'s base
functions for Pearson and Spearman (routine applied benchmarks).

\emph{Computational scope.} The modular design (Section~\ref{sec:design},
3.1) deliberately avoids a full factorial --- which, with the axes
considered, would easily exceed several thousand cells --- given that
dCor, $D$, and $\tau^\ast$ have $O(n^2)$ or worse cost and their
independence tests require permutation; \citet{chatterjee2021} himself
reports competing tests that failed to converge at large sample sizes.
Instead, each module (Table~\ref{tab:modules}) fixes its own minimal
set of cells needed to answer its question. The number of replications
$R$ actually used varies by module according to the computational cost
of the test involved --- on the order of $R=1000$ for modules that only
require $\xi_n$'s own asymptotic test (H1, H2, H5), following the order
used by \citealp{gao2024} and \citealp{dalitz2024}, and $R=300$--$500$
where costlier permutation tests are also computed (H3, H4) --- and is
reported explicitly in the corresponding results table in each case,
together with $B=20$ additional tie-breaks per dataset in the modules
with ties in the covariate (H2b, H2c). Higher-order interactions
between modules, beyond those planned, remain a possible extension of
the reproducibility repository (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}).

\emph{Seed policy.} Each cell is run with a fixed, documented seed
(\texttt{set.seed()}), following the same pattern already used in
AssumptionsLab's Monte Carlo validations: a fixed seed, a versioned
script, and raw results (\texttt{.csv}) committed alongside the script
that produced them, so that any reader can reproduce exactly the same
values.

\emph{Reproducibility repository.} The complete code (data-generating
mechanisms, calibration of $\sigma$, computation of the three
statistics, and generation of Tables~\ref{tab:validation}--\ref{tab:h5b}
and Table~\ref{tab:realdata}) is
published in a dedicated public repository,
\url{https://github.com/ArchieJamDev/ChatterjeeXi-Simulation}, under
\texttt{simulation/}, with its own \texttt{README} of execution
instructions --- kept separate from the manuscript's source code
(\texttt{paper/}) in the same repository.

\section{Results} \label{sec:results}

\subsection{Validation}

Before interpreting any substantive result, we verify that the
implementation reproduces the cases with a known analytical solution:
the exact value of $\xi(X,Y)$ for the equicorrelated normal family of
\citet[Eq.~33]{ansari2026}, $\xi(X,Y) = \frac{3}{\pi}
\arcsin\!\big(\tfrac{1+\rho^2}{2}\big) - \tfrac{1}{2}$ for standard
bivariate normal $(X,Y)$ with correlation $\rho$. For the linear model
$Y = X + \sigma E$ with $X, E \sim N(0,1)$ independent
($\rho = 1/\sqrt{1+\sigma^2}$), Table~\ref{tab:validation} compares
that exact value against our own implementation's large-sample Monte
Carlo estimate ($n=10^5$). The maximum observed difference is $0.002$,
below the tolerance threshold --- the validation gate \emph{is
passed} for the normal family. A closer look at
\citet[Section 5.1]{ansari2026} shows that, unlike the normal case,
that section does not provide an analogous closed form for the
Student-$t$ family in general --- it only proves continuity for the
entire elliptical class, without a numerical reference value against
which to compare. Moreover, this study's own $t$ condition ($X, E \sim
t_{\text{df}}$ independent, $Y=X+\sigma E$; Section~\ref{sec:design})
does not correspond to the bivariate elliptical construction that
theorem requires --- a sum of two independent $t$ variables is not, in
general, itself $t$-distributed as a pair $(X,Y)$. The validation of
the $t$ family against a closed form, as originally planned, is
therefore not possible with this design, and that expectation is
withdrawn: the $t$ condition in Table~\ref{tab:h3} is reported with the
same epistemic status as the non-elliptical families (uniform,
exponential, mixture) --- internal consistency of the simulation, not
external verification against a known exact value.

The absolute magnitude of the bias decreases systematically with $n$
in both regimes, as predicted by H1 --- the signed bias itself, in the
moderate regime, is not monotone (it flips sign between $n=2800$ and
$n=3500$, already within Monte Carlo noise, Table~\ref{tab:h1}).
Notably, at $n=20$ the theoretical bound ($0.857$) lies above both true
values and therefore does not actively constrain the estimator in
either case --- and yet a substantial bias is observed in both. This
confirms H1's conditional reformulation: finite-sample bias does not
depend solely on \citet{dalitz2024}'s algebraic ceiling, which only
operates near $\xi=1$; an additional bias mechanism exists even at
moderate dependence regimes.

The comparison between regimes further confirms, now in a directly
verifiable way, the part of H1 that predicted a particularly marked
reduction when $\xi$ is high: at every sample size up to $n=2800$, the
high regime's bias is larger in magnitude than the moderate regime's
($-0.1225$ vs.\ $-0.0786$ at $n=20$, a difference of more than $50\%$;
the pattern holds without exception through $n=2800$). At $n=3500$,
however, both biases are extremely small ($-0.0003$ in the high regime
vs.\ $0.0006$ in the moderate one) and the comparison flips sign --- a
difference of no practical relevance given how tiny both values are,
but one that should be reported precisely rather than generalized as
holding "at every" sample size. The Monte Carlo standard error further
allows a more precise reading of how far the bias is from zero in each
cell: in the moderate regime, the bias at $n=2000$--$3500$ lies within
$1$--$2$ standard errors of zero (genuinely indistinguishable from
zero within this simulation's error), but in the high regime the bias
at those same sample sizes ($-0.0007$, SE $= 0.0001$) remains several
times its own standard error --- small in absolute magnitude, but not
yet indistinguishable from zero. The high regime not only starts with
a larger bias: it also takes longer to vanish into sampling noise.

H1 also explicitly predicted an effect on MSE, and here the pattern is
not as simple as that of the bias: the high regime's MSE is, in fact,
\emph{smaller} than the moderate regime's at every sample size
($0.0178$ vs.\ $0.0221$ at $n=20$; $0.00001$ vs.\ $0.0001$ at $n=3500$),
despite its larger bias in absolute value. This is consistent with MSE
combining squared bias and variance, and suggests that $\xi_n$'s
variance is small enough in the high regime to offset its larger
bias --- a reminder that not every error metric orders regimes the
same way, and that H1 should not be read as predicting a uniform
ordering of bias and MSE.

The sweep was extended to $n=2800$ and $n=3500$ --- sample sizes common
in large-scale survey research, and which deliberately include
\texttt{bfi}'s nominal sample size (Section~\ref{sec:realdata}; the
complete-case pairs analyzed there are somewhat smaller, $n=2694$ to
$2757$) --- to
directly answer the question of what happens beyond $n=2000$; in the
moderate regime (the relevant one for interpreting
Section~\ref{sec:realdata}, whose estimated $\xi_n$ values are low to
moderate, Table~\ref{tab:realdata}), the bias at $n=2800$ is
indistinguishable from zero within the reported Monte Carlo error.
(Here $n$ always denotes the number of \emph{subjects}, or
observations $(X_i,Y_i)$, a dimension distinct from and independent of
the number of items $k$ averaged per subject in H2d --- $n=2800$ means
2800 people, not 2800 item responses.)

purpose of mitigating $\xi_n$'s tie cost specifically, a moderate
number of items ($k\approx 10$) already captures most of the available
benefit under the conditions examined here ($n=100$, a 6-category
scale, a single latent trait, independent item measurement error);
instruments with many more items (300--400, as in some real
personality or interest inventories) might be justified for other
psychometric reasons (reliability, content validity), but do not
appear to provide an important additional benefit for mitigating
$\xi_n$'s ties specifically under these conditions --- whether that
additional benefit appears under another distribution, sample size, or
item factor structure can neither be ruled out nor claimed from this
design.

pattern is consistent, at an accessible sample scale, with the
theoretical prediction that $\xi_n$ loses ground against signals that
shrink faster than its own detection threshold --- exactly the
mechanism that motivates H5b. With only three sample sizes and two
structures, this experiment does not aim to \emph{establish} the
minimax rate robustly --- that is the province of \citet{auddy2024}'s
theoretical proof --- but rather to show that the asymmetry they
predict is empirically detectable even with a modest design. Note also
that this module reports only $\xi_n$'s own power under the two decay
rates; it does not include dCor, $\tau^\ast$, Pearson, or Spearman, so
it cannot by itself establish that $\xi_n$'s detection threshold
differs empirically from its competitors', as H5b's original wording
suggested --- only that $\xi_n$'s own behavior is consistent with the
asymmetry the theory predicts for $\xi_n$ specifically.

\section{Real-data illustration} \label{sec:realdata}

Following AJS's recommendation to illustrate methods with real,
interesting data rather than trivial examples, $\xi_n$ and its
benchmark measures are applied to the \texttt{bfi} dataset from the
\pkg{psych} package \citep{revelle2024psych}, originating in the SAPA
project \citep{revelle2010sapa}: 2800 subjects, 25 Likert-type
personality items (1--6 scale, five Big Five facets), plus age
(continuous), gender (binary), and education level (ordinal, 1--5). It
was chosen specifically because it combines, within a single real
dataset of considerable size, the three variable types of the
simulation design (Section~\ref{sec:design}), making it possible to
show H1--H5's interpretive cautions in an applied case, not only under
simulated conditions.

Three illustrative pairs are examined, chosen to represent each type
of variable comparison in the design:

\begin{description}
\item[Continuous--continuous.] Age against a neuroticism summary
  score, to explore whether the relationship is genuinely monotone
  (where Pearson/Spearman should suffice) or whether $\xi_n$ detects
  additional non-monotone structure.
\item[Ordinal--ordinal.] Two individual items from the same facet
  (Likert 1--6), as a direct applied case of H2a--c: $\xi_n$ is
  reported under different random tie-breaks, with the resulting
  variability corresponding specifically to H2b (tie-breaking
  randomization when the first item is treated as the predictor).
\item[Binary--continuous.] Gender against an extraversion summary
  score, illustrating H2a's binary case.
\end{description}

For each pair we report $\xi_n(X,Y)$ and $\xi_n(Y,X)$ --- given
$\xi_n$'s directionality (Section~\ref{sec:framework}) --- along with
dCor, $\tau^\ast$, Pearson, and Spearman, and discuss the differences
in light of the findings in Section~\ref{sec:results}: for instance,
whether $n=2800$ is large enough for finite-sample bias (H1) to be
negligible, or whether the number of Likert item categories (H2)
warrants caution when interpreting $\xi_n$'s point estimate relative
to its uncertainty interval.

\section{Discussion} \label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{What was confirmed, what was refined, and what was unexpected}

The five hypotheses, grounded in already-established properties of
$\xi_n$ (Section~\ref{sec:framework}), received real evidence in every
case, though not uniformly confirmatory: two of them (H1, H3) required
extending the original design to be tested in full, and one (H4)
produced a genuinely unexpected finding.

H1 was confirmed in its conditional form --- and now also in the
specific prediction that was originally only asserted without being
directly tested: finite-sample bias is real, decreases with $n$, does
not depend solely on \citet{dalitz2024}'s algebraic bound (substantial
bias exists even when that bound does not actively constrain the
estimator), and is larger in magnitude under a high dependence regime
than under a moderate one at every sample size up to $n=2800$ ---
though both biases are already extremely small and the comparison
reverses at $n=3500$ (Table~\ref{tab:h1}). H2, disaggregated into
H2a--d, showed that ties in the response and in the predictor are
distinct mechanisms with distinct consequences: pure variance in one
case, variance plus tie-breaking randomization in the other
(Tables~\ref{tab:h2a}--\ref{tab:h2b}), and a joint effect
(Table~\ref{tab:h2c}) that is not their sum. H2d showed that the
standard psychometric practice of aggregating items into a composite
score attenuates this problem, though with markedly diminishing
returns: most of the reduction is already achieved with relatively few
items, and instruments with hundreds of items add only a modest further
reduction, because the room for improvement becomes bounded by sample
size rather than by number of items. H3, once evaluated on the five
metrics the hypothesis itself promised (bias, variance, Type I error,
and power, not just the point estimate), turned out more nuanced than
the initial comparison suggested: $\xi_n$ showed a smaller range of
\emph{point estimates} across families than dCor, but it was dCor that
showed smaller \emph{bias} in absolute magnitude, and both exhibited
comparable variance (Table~\ref{tab:h3}) --- neither measure dominates
the other across all dimensions simultaneously. $\xi_n$'s invariance
property itself, in contrast, held without qualification in the
isolated sub-experiment that keeps the base joint distribution fixed
(Table~\ref{tab:h3invar}) --- the comparison across distinct generating
families and invariance under monotone transformations turned out to
be, as H3's reformulation warned, related but not identical questions.
H5a and H5b were consistent, quite sharply for a simulation study with
modest computational resources, with \citet{auddy2024}'s theoretical
asymmetry between $\xi_n$'s $n^{-1/4}$ detection threshold and the
$n^{-1/2}$ optimal parametric rate (Table~\ref{tab:h5b}) --- without
that limited design (three sample sizes, two structures) claiming to
establish that rate robustly on its own.

H4 was the hypothesis with the most unexpected finding. The pattern
already documented by \citet{chatterjee2021} and \citet{gao2024} was
confirmed --- $\xi_n$ less powerful than dCor and $\tau^\ast$ for
linear and heteroskedastic relationships, more powerful for
oscillatory ones --- but the circular structure revealed that dCor and
$\tau^\ast$ do not behave as a homogeneous block of "alternatives to
$\xi_n$": it is $\tau^\ast$, not dCor, that dominates in that
particular geometry (Table~\ref{tab:h4}). This suggests that the
choice of benchmark matters as much as the choice of $\xi_n$ itself,
and that recommendations treating "distance- and copula-based
measures" as interchangeable with one another should be revisited case
by case according to the expected dependence geometry.

\subsection{A practical guide for psychology researchers}

The central message of this study, aimed at the audience that
motivated its design (Section~\ref{sec:introduction}), can be
summarized as follows: $\xi_n$ should be interpreted as a
\emph{directional} measure of how well $Y$ is determined by $X$, with
specific advantages for certain functional patterns and specific costs
under ties, discretization, weak dependence, and certain association
geometries --- not as a measure universally superior or inferior to
Pearson, Spearman, dCor, or $\tau^\ast$. Concretely:

\begin{itemize}
\item With small $n$ ($<100$) and suspected high dependence, expect
  non-negligible downward bias (H1); with $n$ on the order of
  thousands (as in \texttt{bfi}), bias is practically nil.
\item With individual Likert items, especially with few categories,
  expect higher variance --- and, if the covariate is the discretized
  variable, part of that variance comes only from random tie-breaking,
  not from the sample (H2a--c); aggregating items into a composite
  score helps, with most of the benefit already achieved with
  relatively few items (H2d).
\item A low value of $\xi_n$ should not be interpreted as "no
  association" without also checking Pearson or Spearman --- the
  illustration in Section~\ref{sec:realdata} (Age--Neuroticism) shows
  exactly this scenario with real data.
\item If an oscillatory or functionally complex relationship from $X$
  to $Y$ is suspected, $\xi_n$ is probably more sensitive than the
  alternatives; but not every non-monotone relationship favors $\xi_n$
  equally --- in Table~\ref{tab:h4}, $\xi_n$ clearly dominates for the
  sinusoidal structure, but not for the quadratic one (also
  non-monotone), where dCor detects better. If a smooth, monotone,
  circular, or heteroskedastic relationship is suspected, dCor or
  $\tau^\ast$ will likely detect better (H4).
\item Near independence, $\xi_n$ requires substantially larger sample
  sizes than under moderate or high dependence regimes to reach
  reasonable power (H5a--b) --- a relevant consideration when planning
  a study's sample size.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Limitations}

As explicitly stated from the introduction onward, this study covers
\emph{typical}, non-adversarial conditions; \citet{bucher2025}'s
worst-case results remain outside its scope by design, not by
omission. Monte Carlo replications varied across modules ($R=50$ to
$1000$) according to each test's computational cost --- dCor's Type I
error and power in H3, which require its permutation test,
deliberately use the lowest $R$ in the entire study, limited by the
memory available on this machine, while $\xi_n$'s own asymptotic tests
in H1, H2, and H5 use $R=1000$ ---, so estimate precision is not
uniform across tables. Most modules use a single reference cell
(normal shape, linear structure, or $n=100$, depending on the module)
to isolate the effect of interest, following the modular design of
Section~\ref{sec:design}; the full factorial matrix of higher-order
interactions among the five factors was not explored, and remains a
possible extension of the reproducibility repository. For the
Student-$t$ family, no closed form analogous to the normal case exists
in \citet{ansari2026} with this design (Section~\ref{sec:results},
Validation); H3's results for that family should be read, like those
for uniform/exponential/mixture, as internally consistent, not as
externally validated against a known exact value.

\subsection{Future directions}

The blindness to possible non-monotonicity documented in the
Introduction is not limited to bivariate coefficients: systematically
exploring what happens when a structural equation model's true path
coefficients are themselves non-monotone --- and whether $\xi_n$-based
diagnostics can detect that misspecification better than the usual
fit diagnostics --- is a natural extension of this work toward path
analysis and second-order models, and is explicitly left as a future
line of research, distinct from this article.

\section{Conclusion} \label{sec:conclusion}

This article subjected $\xi_n$, \citet{chatterjee2021}'s correlation
coefficient, to a Monte Carlo simulation design organized by modules
and grounded in already-established properties of the estimator ---
not an unguided exploratory sweep --- crossing sample size, variable
type, distribution shape, dependence geometry, and dependence regime.
To the best of our knowledge, none of these crossings previously
existed in the literature on $\xi_n$ (Section~\ref{sec:introduction}),
and each
one reveals behavior with concrete practical consequences for anyone
analyzing psychological data: finite-sample bias that decreases with
$n$ but does not depend solely on the known algebraic bound; distinct,
non-additive costs of ties depending on whether they affect the
predictor, the response, or both, attenuable through composite scores
with diminishing returns; a relationship with dCor across changes in
distribution shape more nuanced than expected --- $\xi_n$ more stable
in point estimate, but not uniformly so in bias or variance --- with
$\xi_n$'s invariance property itself holding without qualification in
an isolated sub-experiment; a dependence geometry that determines
which statistic detects best, with dCor and $\tau^\ast$ unexpectedly
diverging from each other in the circular case; and empirical evidence,
consistent with \citet{auddy2024}'s theoretical result, of $\xi_n$'s
own detection threshold weakening near independence relative to the
optimal parametric rate expected away from it, though this study
compares $\xi_n$ only against itself under two decay rates, not against
its competitors. The real-data illustration with personality data
(\texttt{bfi}) reproduced two of
these findings directly on applied, not just simulated, data. Taken
together, these results support the study's central message: $\xi_n$
is a valuable, directional tool for detecting arbitrary functional
dependence, but its responsible use in psychological research requires
attending to sample size, variable type, and the expected dependence
geometry --- not treating it as a universal replacement for classical
association coefficients.
